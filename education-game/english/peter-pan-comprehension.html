Peter Pan — Two Extracts (The Fight Scene)

J M Barrie (1860–1937)

PETER: Down, boys, and at them!

5The BOYS leap from their concealment and the clash of arms resounds through the vessel. Man to man, the PIRATES are the stronger, but they are unnerved by the suddenness of the onslaught, (10) and they scatter, thus enabling their opponents to hunt in couples and choose their quarry.

(13)Some are hurled into the lagoon; others are dragged from dark recesses. There is no boy whose weapon is not reeking, except SLIGHTLY, who runs about with a lantern, counting, always counting, the pirates. (15)

WENDY: (meeting MICHAEL in a moment's lull) Oh, Michael, stay with me, protect me!

MICHAEL: (reeling) Wendy, I've killed a pirate!

WENDY: It's awful, awful. (20)

MICHAEL: No, it isn't, I like it, I like it.

MICHAEL casts himself into the group of boys who are encircling HOOK. Again and again, they close upon him and again and again he hews a clear space.

HOOK: Back, back, you mice! It's Hook; do you like him? (25)

HOOK lifts up MICHAEL with his claw and uses him as a shield.

A terrible voice breaks in.

PETER: Put up your swords, boys. This man is mine.

HOOK shakes MICHAEL off his claw as if he were a drop of water, and these two antagonists face each other for their final bout. They measure swords at arms' length, make a sweeping motion with them, and bringing the points to the deck, rest their hands upon the hilts. (30)

HOOK: (with curling lip) So, Pan, this is all your doing!

PETER: Ay, Jas Hook, it is all my doing.

HOOK: Proud and insolent youth, prepare to meet thy doom. (35)

PETER: Dark and sinister man, have this.

HOOK or PETER this time! They fall to without another word. PETER is a rare swordsman, and parries with dazzling rapidity, sometimes before the other can make his stroke.

HOOK, if not quite so nimble in wrist play, has the advantage of a yard or two in reach,(40) but though they close he cannot make the final blow with his claw, which seems to find nothing to tear at. He does not, especially in the most heated moments, quite see PETER, who is to his eyes, now blurred or opened clearly for the first time, less like a boy than a mote of dust dancing in the sun.(45)

By some imperceptible stroke, HOOK's sword is whipped from his grasp, and when he stoops to raise it, a little foot is on its blade. There is no deep gash on HOOK, but he is suffering torment as from innumerable blows.

(50)"Down, boys, and at them!" Peter's voice rang out; and in another moment, the clash of arms was resounding through the ship.

(53)Had the pirates kept together it is certain that they would have won; but the onset came when they were still unstrung, and they ran hither and thither, striking wildly, each thinking himself the last survivor of the crew.(55)

Man to man they were the stronger; but they fought on the defensive only, which enabled the boys to hunt in pairs and choose their quarry. Some of the miscreants leapt into the sea; others hid in dark recesses, where they were found by Slightly, who did not fight, but ran about with a lantern which he flashed in their faces,(60) so that they were half blinded and fell as an easy prey to the reeking swords of the other boys.

There was little sound to be heard but the clang of weapons, an occasional screech or splash, and Slightly monotonously counting — five—six—seven—eight—nine—ten—eleven pirates.(65)

I think all were gone when a group of savage boys surrounded Hook, who seemed to have a charmed life, as he kept them at bay in that circle of fire. They had done for his dogs, but this man alone seemed to be a match for them all.(70)

Again and again, they closed upon him, and again and again he hewed a clear space. He had lifted up one boy with his hook, and was using him as a shield, when another, who had just passed his sword through Mullins, sprang into the fray.

(75)"Put up your swords, boys," cried the newcomer, "This man is mine."

Thus, suddenly Hook found himself face to face with Peter. The others drew back and formed a ring around them.

For a long time, the two enemies looked at one another, Hook shuddering slightly, and Peter with the strange smile upon his face.(80)

"So, Pan," said Hook at last, "This is all your doing."

"Ay, James Hook," came the stern answer, "It is all my doing."

"Proud and insolent youth," said Hook, "Prepare to meet thy doom,"

"Dark and sinister man," Peter answered, "Have this."(85)

Without more words they fell to, and for a time there was no advantage to either blade. Peter was a superb swordsman and parried with dazzling rapidity; with lunges that got past his foe's defence, but his shorter reach stood him in ill stead, and he could not drive the steel home.(90)

Hook, scarcely his inferior in brilliancy, but not quite so nimble in wrist play, forced him back by the weight of his onset, hoping suddenly to end all with a favourite thrust; but to his astonishment he found this thrust turned aside again and again.(95)

Then he sought to close and give the final blow with his iron hook, which all this time had been pawing the air; but Peter doubled under it and, lunging fiercely, pierced him in the ribs. At the sight of his own blood, whose peculiar colour, you remember, was offensive to him, the sword fell from Hook's hand, and he was at Peter's mercy.(100)

💡 Why does this matter?

When Barrie wrote the novel, he streamlined the action. Wendy and Michael's conversation ("I've killed a pirate!" / "It's awful, awful" / "No, it isn't, I like it!") humanises the characters and adds humour and contrast in the play — but would slow down the pace of the novel's fast-moving narrative. The playwright chose to include it; the novelist chose to cut it. That's a structural choice worth discussing in evaluative questions.

Practise Answering — Try These!

Practice Question A — 2 marks (Contextual)

How does the presentation of Peter Pan differ between the play extract and the novel excerpt? Use evidence from both texts.
Model Answer:

In the play, Peter appears with dramatic suddenness, his entry announced only by "a terrible voice" before he commands the stage. This theatrical entrance creates immediate tension.

In the novel, Peter enters more mysteriously as "the newcomer" — the narrator withholds his name, building suspense before the reveal. The novel also describes his "strange smile upon his face" during the stare-down with Hook, emphasising his eerie, supernatural calm — something the play conveys through stage direction rather than description.

Practice Question B — 3 marks (Evaluative)

Do you think the play or the novel creates a more exciting fight scene? Use evidence from both texts to explain your view.
Model Answer (novel is more exciting — one possible view):

I think the novel creates a more exciting fight scene because it provides more vivid physical detail. For example, the novel describes Peter "lunging fiercely" and "piercing Hook in the ribs" — a concrete, physical action that the play omits, replacing it with the more theatrical but less visceral image of the sword being "whipped" away.

Furthermore, the novel builds tension through the narrator's voice. The phrase "I think all were gone" makes the narrator sound like a witness unsure of what's happening — this uncertainty makes the reader feel as if the battle is unpredictable and dangerous. The play, by contrast, uses stage directions which feel more controlled and managed.

However, the play creates excitement through dialogue: Michael's "Wendy, I've killed a pirate!" followed by "I like it, I like it!" adds a disturbing, unexpected humour that shocks the audience — something the novel cannot replicate.

Practice Question C — 4 marks (Evaluative)

Explain what kind of character Peter Pan is. Use evidence from both extracts to support your answer.
Model Answer:

1. Courageous and commanding: Peter shows natural leadership when he orders "Put up your swords, boys. This man is mine." This shows confidence and bravery — he is willing to face Hook alone without hesitation.

2. Supremely skilled: Peter is described as "a rare swordsman" who "parried with dazzling rapidity, sometimes before the other can make his stroke." This suggests his reflexes are almost supernatural — he fights instinctively.

3. Mysterious/otherworldly: Hook struggles to see Peter clearly, who appears to his eyes as "less like a boy than a mote of dust dancing in the sun" — suggesting Peter is barely human, more like a spirit or force of nature. This links to his magical, immortal nature as a boy who never grows up.

4. Calm and fearless: In the novel, Peter faces Hook with a "strange smile upon his face" while Hook is shuddering. This calm in the face of danger suggests Peter is not afraid of death — which makes him both admirable and unsettling.

11+ Exam Tips for Comprehension
01
Count the marks

2 marks = 2 points. 4 marks = 4 points. Never write one point for a 4-mark question.

02
Quote the text

Use exact words in speech marks. "The text says '...' " earns marks. Vague references don't.

03
P.E.E. always

Point → Evidence → Explain. Every evaluative paragraph needs all three steps.

04
Both texts

Comparison questions must reference BOTH extracts. One text = half the marks maximum.

05
Vocabulary precision

Use the text's own words. Echo the author's vocabulary — it shows careful reading.

06
Read the question twice

Underline the key task word: "explain", "compare", "how does". Each demands a different type of answer.
